# Research Strand 02 — Technical Analysis: The Empirical Record

**Prepared:** 10 November 2026
**Scope:** Layer 2 of the proposed app (chart pattern fitting, Fibonacci retracement, support/resistance, Elliott wave → bullish/bearish/neutral trend classification), plus the methodological traps a 0–100 blended score will face.
**Posture:** This strand weighs the literature as it falls. Several of the named techniques have no credible supporting evidence; that is reported as a finding, not softened.
**Evidence labels:** `[ACADEMIC]` peer-reviewed or working paper with method disclosed · `[PRACTITIONER]` blog/vendor/marketing · `[VENDOR-BACKTEST]` commercial backtest with commercial interest.

> **Search budget note:** This strand ran ~30 web searches before the turn's shared search budget (200 calls across five parallel agents) was exhausted; remaining work was completed by direct fetch of already-surfaced URLs. Three sources could not be retrieved (full text of Savin/Weller/Zvingelis 2007; Osler 2000 full PDF; Tsinaslanidis/Voukelatos Fibonacci ESWA 2021 full text). Where only an abstract or press release was obtained, that is stated inline.

## 1. CHART PATTERN RECOGNITION

### 1.1 The foundational paper

**CLAIM:** Lo, Mamaysky & Wang established that algorithmically detected chart patterns carry *statistically detectable incremental information* about the next-day conditional return distribution — but the authors explicitly declined to claim tradable profit.
**SOURCE:** Andrew W. Lo, Harry Mamaysky, Jiang Wang, "Foundations of Technical Analysis: Computational Algorithms, Statistical Inference, and Empirical Implementation," *Journal of Finance* 55(4): 1705–1765, 2000. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/0022-1082.00265 · PDF: https://web.mit.edu/wangj/www/pap/LoMamayskyWang00.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate (for informativeness) / No credible evidence (for profitability — not tested)
**Detail:** Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression smooths prices; local extrema are classified into 10 patterns (head-and-shoulders, inverse H&S, broadening top/bottom, triangle top/bottom, rectangle top/bottom, double top/bottom). 38-trading-day rolling windows, 3-day detection lag. CRSP daily NYSE/AMEX + Nasdaq, 1962–1996, seven 5-year subperiods, 50 randomly selected stocks each. Chi-square goodness-of-fit and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests on decile distributions of standardised 1-day conditional returns.
**Results:** NYSE/AMEX — goodness-of-fit significant for 7 of 10 patterns; KS significant for only 5 (HS, BBOT, RTOP, RBOT, DTOP). The paper's own word for the overall support is "mixed." Nasdaq — all 10 patterns significant at 5% on both tests, with notably larger statistics (34.1–92.1 vs. 12.0–51.0).
**Authors' own caveats (important):** the bandwidth multiplier (0.3 × cross-validated h\*) is described by the authors as ad hoc, chosen because technical analysts said the statistically optimal bandwidth over-smoothed; the IID assumption is called implausible for financial data; informativeness is a weaker test than profitability; transaction costs are not analysed.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the single strongest citation available for "chart patterns contain information," and it is *much* weaker than it is usually represented to be. It tests a one-day distributional difference, on 1962–1996 data, with a hand-tuned smoothing parameter, and the authors state in the paper that it does not imply excess trading profits. Do not cite it as evidence the app's pattern layer predicts returns. Note also: the effect was concentrated in Nasdaq (small, illiquid, high-spread names in that era) — exactly where costs eat the most.

### 1.2 Head-and-shoulders, tested for money

**CLAIM:** Automated head-and-shoulders trading in FX lost money at every tested frequency, before and after costs, and filtering rarely rescued it.
**SOURCE:** M. A. H. Dempster & C. M. Jones, "Can Technical Pattern Trading Be Profitably Automated? 2. The Head & Shoulders," Centre for Financial Research, Judge Institute of Management Studies, University of Cambridge, December 1998. https://www.statslab.cam.ac.uk/~mrt31//CFR/publications/content/papers/H&S-part2.pdf `[ACADEMIC — working paper]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate (single currency pair, small test sample)
**Detail:** GBP/USD tick data 1989–1997, aggregated to 1/60/240/480-min and daily bars. Local-extrema H&S detection; entry at close of neckline break; exit on 30-pip trailing stop or 3× formation time. Costs 10 pips round-turn plus 2.5–5 pips slippage. Mean slippage-adjusted profit per trade: −31 (daily), −34 (480m), −29 (240m), −28 (60m), −37 (1m) pips. Losses exceeded the maximum 20-pip slippage deduction, so the strategy was loss-making *gross* as well. Discriminant-analysis filters improved sample performance and ~69% of test cases, but test subsamples were tiny (2 of 62 trades retained at 240m; 2 of 9 at daily).
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The most-named pattern in retail education is the one with a published, explicit, cost-aware loss. If the app surfaces H&S as a bullish/bearish signal, it is surfacing something with a negative published expectation in at least one liquid market.

**CLAIM:** The head-and-shoulders pattern in US equities was unprofitable, and the traders who act on it look like noise traders.
**SOURCE:** C. L. Osler, "Identifying Noise Traders: The Head-and-Shoulders Pattern in U.S. Equities," FRBNY Staff Report No. 42, February 1998. https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr42.pdf `[ACADEMIC — central bank staff report]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate–Strong
**Detail:** Mean profit per position −0.24% over ~10 business days, vs. −0.03% in simulated random-return data; the difference was not statistically significant. A second test found a slight loss tendency. Separately, H&S-triggered trading *did* move prices — day-after returns ~0.06pp above the simulated baseline — but the effect reversed within days and was statistically gone by the end of week two. Osler notes H&S-driven volume is roughly one-fifth to one-quarter of a day's volume when active. Her conclusion: these traders are high-volume, unprofitable positive-feedback traders who mistake noise for information.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** There is a genuine microstructure footprint — enough people trade H&S that it is visible in volume and in a 1-day price impact. But the impact is (a) smaller than transaction costs and (b) mean-reverting. An app that tells retail users "H&S detected → bearish" is putting them on the documented losing side of a documented noise-trading pattern.

### 1.3 The one notable positive result

**CLAIM:** Savin, Weller & Zvingelis report that H&S patterns predicted excess returns in US equities (~5–7%/yr risk-adjusted) while *not* supporting a standalone pattern-only trading strategy.
**SOURCE:** N. E. Savin, Paul Weller, Janis Zvingelis, "The Predictive Power of 'Head-and-Shoulders' Price Patterns in the U.S. Stock Market," *Journal of Financial Econometrics* 5(2): 243–265, 2007. https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jfinec/v5yi2p243-265.html `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak–Moderate (full text not retrieved; abstract-level only)
**Detail from abstract:** S&P 500 and Russell 2000, 1990–1999. They use the LMW kernel-regression detector *modified with filters based on patterns a technical analyst identified as typical*. Little or no support for a stand-alone pattern strategy; strong claimed predictive power for excess returns; ~5–7%/yr risk-adjusted when combined with the market portfolio. The abstract does not state the horizon, the robustness checks, or whether returns are net of costs — I could not retrieve the full text to verify.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the best card in the deck for chart patterns, and it comes with three problems: (1) the detector was *tuned by a human expert on the same era of data*, which is a textbook in-sample selection channel; (2) the sample is a single decade (1990s) and has not, as far as this search found, been replicated out-of-sample on post-2000 US data; (3) the authors themselves say the stand-alone strategy does not work. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a foundation.

### 1.4 Other pattern evidence and the survey verdict

**CLAIM:** Across the surveyed chart-pattern literature, results are inconsistent by pattern, market and period, and the studies are methodologically weak in specific, consistent ways.
**SOURCE:** Cheol-Ho Park & Scott H. Irwin, "The Profitability of Technical Analysis: A Review," AgMAS Project Research Report 2004-04, University of Illinois. https://farmdoc.illinois.edu/assets/marketing/agmas/AgMAS04_04.pdf (later published as "What Do We Know About the Profitability of Technical Analysis?", *Journal of Economic Surveys* 21(4), 2007) `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong (as a survey)
**Detail:** 92 modern studies (1988–Aug 2004) plus 42 early studies (1960–1987). Of the 92: **58 positive, 24 negative, 10 mixed.** On chart patterns specifically: Chang & Osler (1999) found H&S returns of ~13% and ~19%/yr for mark and yen, *but* simple moving-average and momentum rules easily beat them, and adding H&S to those rules raised risk without raising profit — Chang & Osler concluded using H&S was not rational. Dawson & Steeley (2003) found negative average market-adjusted returns on UK data. Curcio et al. (1997) found limited FX profitability, declining over time. Park & Irwin's critical note: **most chart-pattern studies did no parameter optimisation, no out-of-sample testing, and paid little attention to data snooping.**
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The 58-vs-24 headline count is the number most often quoted in favour of TA. It is a count of *studies*, not of validated effects, drawn from a literature the same authors describe as riddled with ex post rule selection. Do not use it as a quality signal.

### 1.5 Machine-learning pattern detection (CNNs on chart images)

**CLAIM:** Published CNN-on-candlestick-image results report implausibly high accuracies and generally do not survive methodological scrutiny; they evaluate classification accuracy, not trading profit.
**SOURCE:** Rosdyana Mangir Irawan Kusuma, Trang-Thi Ho, Wei-Chun Kao, Yu-Yen Ou, Kai-Lung Hua, "Using Deep Learning Neural Networks and Candlestick Chart Representation to Predict Stock Market," arXiv:1903.12258. https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.12258 `[ACADEMIC — preprint]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak (claimed effect not credible as stated)
**Detail:** OHLC rendered as 50×50 / 20×20 candlestick images over 5/10/20-day windows; CNN/ResNet50/VGG16/RF/kNN predicting next-day direction; 50 Taiwanese + 10 Indonesian stocks; train 2000–2016, test 2017–Jun 2018. Headline accuracies **92.2%** (Taiwan) and **92.1%** (Indonesia). On inspection: the "best" configuration appears selected from 12+ configurations evaluated on the test period with no separate validation set; the "independent" test set carries the *same date range* as the test set; results tables are duplicated across markets and internally inconsistent (one row reports 91.0% accuracy while its own sensitivity 81.7% / specificity 91.4% imply ~86.5%); up/down label construction and zero-change handling are undefined; sliding windows straddling the 2016/2017 boundary could leak; class balance is unreported, so accuracy cannot be read against a majority-class baseline; universe selection was performance-conditioned. **No trading simulation, no returns, no costs, no buy-and-hold comparison.**
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** If the team is tempted to train a pattern classifier on chart images and cite literature like this as precedent, the precedent is not sound. A 92% next-day direction accuracy on liquid equities is prima facie evidence of leakage, not of signal. Any in-house version must use purged, embargoed, walk-forward validation (see §7) and must be evaluated on cost-aware P&L, not accuracy.

## 2. TECHNICAL ANALYSIS BROADLY — AND THE DATA-SNOOPING RECKONING

### 2.1 The original positive result

**CLAIM:** Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron found moving-average and trading-range-break rules on the DJIA produced returns inconsistent with four standard null models of stock returns.
**SOURCE:** William Brock, Josef Lakonishok, Blake LeBaron, "Simple Technical Trading Rules and the Stochastic Properties of Stock Returns," *Journal of Finance* 47(5): 1731–1764, 1992. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:47:y:1992:i:5:p:1731-64 `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate in isolation; see §2.2–2.4 for what happened next.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** BLL is the canonical "TA works" citation. It is 34 years old, uses DJIA data ending in 1986, and was deliberately framed by its authors as a test against null models rather than a trading recommendation. It is the *starting point* of the debate, not the conclusion.

### 2.2 Data-snooping-adjusted re-test: BLL survives in-sample, dies out-of-sample

**CLAIM:** BLL's results survive a formal data-snooping correction on their original sample, but the same rule universe shows essentially no economic value in 1987–1996.
**SOURCE:** Ryan Sullivan, Allan Timmermann, Halbert White, "Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap," *Journal of Finance* 54(5): 1647–1691, 1999. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00163 · PDF: https://www.kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** 7,846 rules (filter, moving average, support & resistance, channel breakout, on-balance-volume) over DJIA daily closes 1897–1996 and S&P 500 futures 1984–1996, assessed with White's Reality Check bootstrap. Full 100 years: best rule a 5-day MA, 17.2%/yr, adjusted p = 0.000. BLL's 90-year window: best full-universe rule 18.65%/yr, p = 0.000. BLL's own best rule (50-day VMA, 0.01 band) stays significant in the full sample and in all four in-sample subperiods.
**Then 1987–1996:** best full-universe DJIA rule returns 14.4% but adjusted **p = 0.341**; best BLL-universe rule 8.63%, **p = 0.154**; S&P 500 futures best rule **p = 0.908**, with no rule beating the benchmark. The authors' own summary is that there is scant evidence these rules had economic value in 1987–1996, and of three candidate explanations they favour increased market efficiency (cheaper computing, lower transaction costs, greater liquidity).
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the pivotal finding for a 2026 US-equity product. The rule families the app would use are the same five families STW tested, and their measured edge is an artefact of pre-1987 data. A trend classifier built from these rules should be assumed to have zero expected alpha unless the team demonstrates otherwise on post-2000 data with its own costs.

### 2.3 The strongest modern verdict: costs and false discoveries kill it

**CLAIM:** Applying false-discovery-rate control and realistic costs to the same 7,846-rule universe over 1897–2011, no technical rule shows positive performance from 1962 onward even at zero cost, and earlier profitability vanishes at one-way costs of ~16–70 bps.
**SOURCE:** Pierre Bajgrowicz & Olivier Scaillet, "Technical trading revisited: False discoveries, persistence tests, and transaction costs," *Journal of Financial Economics* 106(3): 473–491, 2012. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X1200116X · Open version: https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/c0c2aa38-f0bf-430e-b994-dbe8bb53fb13/download `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** FDR+/− methodology (Barras–Scaillet–Wermers) instead of a best-rule reality check, so it identifies *portfolios* of rules rather than one winner — a design that is more favourable to TA, with higher power. Monthly rebalancing using only past data. Findings: many outperforming rules pre-1960s before costs; one-way costs of ~16–70 bps eliminate positive performance in the first three subperiods; **from 1962 onward, no positive-performing rules are detected even at zero cost.** Out-of-sample performance is negative in most recent periods even at zero cost. Rule-portfolio turnover is extreme — almost entirely new rules after two rebalancings. Short-lending fees of 5–20 bps erase early-period out-of-sample performance. The authors' conclusion: historical success was a statistical artefact of data snooping, and the predictability could not have been selected or exploited ex ante.
**Authors' own scope limit:** the result applies to these simple rules, on the DJIA, at daily frequency.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the hardest single result against shipping classical TA rules as a return signal in US equities. The turnover finding also matters directly: if the app's "best" rules change every rebalance, any user-facing claim of a stable methodology is false.

### 2.4 Moving averages: the outperformance is pre-1944

**CLAIM:** Moving-average and momentum market-timing rules on the S&P Composite show a structural break around May 1944; outperformance concentrates in the first half of the sample and in a handful of bear episodes, and there is no statistically significant outperformance post-1942.
**SOURCE:** Valeriy Zakamulin, "A Comprehensive Look at the Empirical Performance of Moving Average Trading Strategies," working paper, Dec 2015 rev. https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/SSRN-id2677212.pdf `[ACADEMIC — working paper]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** Four rule families (MOM, P-SMA, P-REMA, double-crossover), lookbacks 1–24 months, rolling and expanding window selection; S&P Composite 1860–2014, out-of-sample 1870–2014, subperiods 1870–1942 and 1942–2014; 0.25% one-way costs. Estimated structural break **May 1944** (95% CI May 1943 – May 1945). Full sample: only MOM and P-REMA (rolling) have Sharpe ratios significantly different from the market at 5%. Second subperiod: Sharpe ratios 7–14% above the market but **no statistically significant outperformance**; all rules below 50% outperformance probability; median outperformance negative for all rules. Outperformance clusters in bear episodes of the 1870s, 1900s, 1930s, 1970s and 2000s. Signals number 2–3× the count of actual market phases (i.e. mostly false). Average directional accuracy ~75% vs. 74% for buy-and-hold.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The 75%-vs-74% figure is the one to internalise. A moving-average trend classifier is barely more accurate than a constant "bullish" label, and most of its signals are false turns. If the app's neutral/bullish/bearish output is MA-driven, its information content over "the market usually goes up" is close to zero outside bear markets.

**CLAIM:** Out-of-sample MA and time-series-momentum timing rules delivered *lower* capital growth than buy-and-hold and at best marginal Sharpe improvement, and the apparent edge is explained by serial dependence rather than skill.
**SOURCE:** Valeriy Zakamulin, "The real-life performance of market timing with moving average and time-series momentum rules," *Journal of Asset Management* 15: 261–278, 2014 (earlier titled "Fooled by Data-Mining"). PDF: https://foro.masdividendos.com/uploads/default/original/2X/a/a1bd5ae7e323496b5072adf8c2e126e31764e306.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** Jan 1926 – Dec 2012 monthly, S&P Composite / DJIA / long- and intermediate-term Treasuries; SMA(k) and MOM(k) for k = 2…24 selected by in-sample Sharpe; one-way costs 0.50% stocks / 0.10% bonds; stationary block bootstrap (mean block 16 months, 1,000 replications) averaged over in-sample lengths of 4–50 years to remove split-point arbitrariness. Backtest-optimal rules were SMA(10) for the S&P and SMA(13) for the DJIA — note that SMA(10) is precisely the rule popularised in practitioner literature, i.e. the best in-sample rule is the one everyone cites. **Split-point fragility:** for 1930–2012 the S&P buy-and-hold Sharpe was 0.11 vs. 0.12 for out-of-sample SMA; moving the split by one year reversed the ranking. Robust results relative to buy-and-hold: S&P SMA mean return **−20%**, volatility −32%, Sharpe +7%; S&P MOM Sharpe ±0%; DJIA SMA −11%, MOM −8%; long bonds SMA −17%, MOM −21%. Only 2 of 8 strategies slightly beat benchmark. **$100 in 1930 → $166,155 buy-and-hold vs. $64,345 (SMA) and $88,871 (MOM).** Bootstrap p-value 0.38 for the S&P SMA rule.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The $166k vs. $64k figure is the number to show any stakeholder who believes trend timing is free. Also: the finding that the *famous* rule is the *backtest-optimal* rule is a direct demonstration of how practitioner consensus is generated by selection, not by evidence.

## 3. MOMENTUM AND TREND FOLLOWING — THE DEFENSIBLE CORE, AND ITS LIMITS

### 3.1 Cross-sectional momentum

**CLAIM:** Buying past 3–12-month winners and shorting past losers earned significant positive returns over 3–12-month holding periods in US equities, not explained by systematic risk or delayed factor reaction, with partial reversal in years 2–3.
**SOURCE:** Narasimhan Jegadeesh & Sheridan Titman, "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers: Implications for Stock Market Efficiency," *Journal of Finance* 48(1): 65–91, 1993. https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v48y1993i1p65-91.html `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong (most-replicated anomaly in the literature)
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP — critical distinction:** This is a **cross-sectional, long–short, portfolio-level, monthly-rebalanced** effect. It says past winners beat past losers *relative to each other*. It does **not** say that a stock with a positive 12-month return will rise. The app's architecture is per-ticker and absolute-direction, so J&T momentum does not transfer to it without being reformulated as a *relative ranking* across the user's universe. Any claim that the app's trend classifier rests on momentum literature is only defensible if the signal is actually relative.

### 3.2 Time-series momentum, and the challenge to it

**CLAIM:** Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen documented time-series momentum — a security's own past 12-month excess return predicting its next-month return — across 58 futures and forward contracts.
**SOURCE:** Tobias J. Moskowitz, Yao Hua Ooi, Lasse Heje Pedersen, "Time series momentum," *Journal of Financial Economics* 104(2): 228–250, 2012. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:104:y:2012:i:2:p:228-250 `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Contested — see immediately below.

**CLAIM:** The TSM result does not survive asset-by-asset testing, appropriate bootstrap critical values, or comparison against a benchmark that requires no predictability at all.
**SOURCE:** Dashan Huang, Jiangyuan Li, Liyao Wang, Guofu Zhou, "Time series momentum: Is it there?", *Journal of Financial Economics* 135(3): 774–794, 2020. https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/6521/ · Working version: https://down.aefweb.net/WorkingPapers/w717.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** Same 55 contracts and same 1985–2015 window as MOP. Univariate regressions: **only 8 of 55 slopes significant at 10%** (3 at 5%); mean in-sample R² = 0.39%; out-of-sample (2000–2015) **45 of 55 assets have negative R²OS**, only 3 significant at 10%, mean R²OS = −0.67%. MOP's pooled 12-month t-statistic of 4.34 is compared against bootstrap 5% critical values of **12.53 (wild)** and **4.83 (pairs)** — i.e. it does not reject the null; with fixed effects the t-statistic falls to 3.37 against critical values 10.13 / 3.93. Without volatility scaling the t-statistic drops to **1.68**. Against a "time-series history" (TSH) benchmark that simply goes long if the full-sample historical mean is positive — requiring *no* predictability — the portfolio-level mean-return difference is 0.14% (p = 0.19) and the four-factor alpha difference 0.10% (p = 0.29) or −0.02% (p = 0.84) under the Asness et al. model. Asset-level Sharpe ratios are near-identical (S&P 500: TSM 0.15 vs. TSH 0.16). Most profit comes from the long legs. Simulations: at the empirically estimated slope (~0.08), TSM and TSH perform the same.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** TSM — the technique closest to a single-asset trend classifier — is the one momentum result that has been most directly challenged, and the challenge is published in the same top journal. Moreover, the benchmark that ties TSM is "be long things that have historically gone up," which is what a buy-and-hold already does. A per-ticker bullish/bearish classifier has no clean academic foundation.

**CLAIM:** A defender's reading is that the corrections make MOP's statistical support marginal rather than absent.
**SOURCE:** Wesley Gray, "Are Trend-Following and Time-Series Momentum Research Results Robust?", Alpha Architect. https://alphaarchitect.com/are-trend-following-and-time-series-momentum-research-results-robust/ `[PRACTITIONER]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak (commentary)
**Detail:** Gray concludes MOP's claims are probably overstated but that trend-following is not "fake news," stresses that one-month-ahead returns are extremely noisy, and notes the Huang et al. paper does not test trend-following's diversification and tail-risk benefits — which he argues are its actual purpose. Worth recording as the steelman.

### 3.3 Trend-following over very long samples (vendor research)

**CLAIM:** An equal-weighted 1/3/12-month TSM strategy across 67 markets delivered a net-of-fee Sharpe ratio of 0.77 over 1880–2013, with a weaker 0.62 over 2000–2013.
**SOURCE:** Brian Hurst, Yao Hua Ooi, Lasse Heje Pedersen, "A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing," AQR Capital Management, Fall 2014. https://oxfordstrat.com/coasdfASD32/uploads/2016/03/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf `[VENDOR-BACKTEST]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak as independent evidence (hindsight-constructed, commercial interest), Moderate as a description of strategy behaviour
**Detail:** Monthly 1880–2013; 29 commodities, 11 equity indices, 15 bond markets, 12 currency pairs (appendix lists 10 currencies — internally inconsistent); volatility-scaled positions, 10% ex-ante portfolio vol target, 3-year rolling covariance. Gross return 14.9%, net 11.2%, realised vol 9.7%, net Sharpe 0.77. Fees: 2% management + 20% performance. Costs by asset class and era (equities 0.34% one-way 1880–1992, 0.11% 1993–2002, 0.06% 2003–2013; pre-1993 costs assumed at 6× current, 1993–2002 at 2×). By decade (net Sharpe): 1970s 1.70 (best), 1910s 0.13 (worst), 1980s 0.96, 1990s 0.98, **2000–2013 0.62**. S&P correlation in that recent window −0.30. A 20% allocation inside 60/40 raised net Sharpe 0.38 → 0.54 and cut max drawdown −62.3% → −50.2%.
**Authors' own caveats:** results are hypothetical and constructed with hindsight; the strategy "would not necessarily have been implementable as described" in the 1880s because the financing markets and equity/bond futures did not exist; pre-futures data uses proxies; future returns may be lower due to AUM growth, high fees, and post-2007 cross-market correlations. **Both authors are AQR principals and the paper recommends an allocation.**
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Even taken at face value this is a *portfolio-level, multi-asset, volatility-targeted, monthly* strategy with a Sharpe under 1 — and it is a trend-following manager's own backtest of a product they sell. It cannot support a claim that per-ticker chart-based trend classification helps a retail options buyer.

### 3.4 Independent evidence that the trend signal has weakened

**CLAIM:** The lag-1 autocorrelation of volatility-normalised futures returns — the mechanism trend-following profits from — fell from about 0.04 in the 1990s to about 0.01 after 2010, and break-even costs for the reference system sit at the low end of realistic cost ranges.
**SOURCE:** Artur Sepp (LGT Bank) & Vladimir Lucic (Marex Solutions / Imperial College London), "The Science and Practice of Trend-Following Systems," arXiv:2607.19497, 20 July 2026. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.19497 `[ACADEMIC — preprint; authors industry-affiliated]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate
**Detail:** Dec 1999 – Jun 2026, 84 futures, net of costs and fees: European EWMA system Sharpe 0.47, American ATR-breakout 0.50, TSMOM 0.55, vs. SG Trend Index 0.47; Ledoit–Wolf p-values 0.96 / 0.82 / 0.62 — none of the differences significant. ~80% correlation with the SG Trend Index; European and American systems correlate ~95% with each other (i.e. the design choices practitioners argue about barely matter). Turnover 300–400%/yr for European and TSMOM, 125–200% for American; reference configuration realises ~1.7%/yr in costs. For the short-memory case, break-even cost ~37–41 bps against a realistic 40–60 bps range — **the alpha is not clearly exploitable after costs**, and market impact is excluded.
**Authors' own caveats:** attribution is in-sample; the sample drift estimate is upward biased and needs debiasing; grid results are sensitivity analysis because the best grid Sharpe overstates out-of-sample; daily-moment Sharpe ratios overstate horizon Sharpe when signals are persistent.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The best available recent, independent-ish estimate puts trend-following's post-cost edge at roughly zero in a market (futures) with far lower frictions than retail equity options. The autocorrelation decay figure is the cleanest single piece of evidence that the underlying phenomenon has weakened.

### 3.5 Momentum's tail risk

**CLAIM:** Momentum suffers infrequent but persistent and severe loss runs, concentrated in post-decline, high-volatility, rebound states.
**SOURCE:** Kent Daniel & Tobias J. Moskowitz, "Momentum Crashes," *Journal of Financial Economics* 122(2): 221–247, 2016 (NBER WP 20439, 2014). https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439 `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** Crashes occur in "panic" states — after market declines, when volatility is high, during rebounds — and are partly forecastable. Authors attribute low expected returns in these states to a conditionally high risk premium on the option-like payoffs of past losers. Their proposed dynamic strategy roughly doubles static momentum's alpha and Sharpe. (Moskowitz discloses an AQR relationship.)
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Even the defensible part of TA has a fat left tail that arrives exactly when a retail user is most likely to be engaged (high volatility, post-crash). Combined with long options positions, a trend classifier that stays bearish into a rebound can produce correlated, total losses. This belongs in the risk disclosure, not only the model.

### 3.6 Post-publication decay

**CLAIM:** Published return predictors decay ~26% out-of-sample before publication and ~58% after publication, implying roughly a 32% publication-driven effect.
**SOURCE:** R. David McLean & Jeffrey Pontiff, "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?", *Journal of Finance* 71(1): 5–32, 2016. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12365 · PDF: https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** 97 cross-sectional predictors from 79 studies. Long–short returns fall from 58.2 bps/month in-sample to ~40 bps post-sample pre-publication (−26%, treated as an upper bound on statistical bias) to ~26 bps post-publication (−58%). Predictors with higher in-sample returns decay more; those concentrated in costly-to-arbitrage stocks (high idiosyncratic risk, low liquidity) retain more; post-publication volume and short interest rise in predictor stocks.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Classical TA patterns were published in the 1930s–1990s and are taught to millions. Under this decay model, the expected residual edge in widely known, free, visually-identifiable rules is the floor of the distribution, not the middle. The subset that retains return is the subset that is *expensive to arbitrage* — which for a retail options product means wide spreads and bad fills, i.e. the cost channel eats it.

## 4. SUPPORT AND RESISTANCE — THE ONE REAL MECHANISM

**CLAIM:** Stop-loss and take-profit order *requests* in FX cluster strongly at round numbers, and the asymmetry between the two order types supplies a microstructure mechanism for both reversal at round levels and acceleration after a break.
**SOURCE:** Carol L. Osler, "Currency Orders and Exchange Rate Dynamics: An Explanation for the Predictive Success of Technical Analysis," *Journal of Finance* 58(5): 1791–1819, 2003 (FRBNY Staff Report 125). https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1540-6261.00588 · https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr125.html `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong (for the mechanism)
**Detail:** Order-book data from a large FX dealing bank. Requested execution rates of both order types cluster at round numbers; the clustering pattern differs between stop-loss and take-profit orders and between stop-loss buys and sells. Take-profit orders tend to *oppose* trends (absorbing them near round levels); stop-loss orders *intensify* trends. Osler maps this directly onto the two classic TA predictions: trends reverse at predictable levels, and trends accelerate once levels are crossed. (Sample size, period and pairs were not retrievable from the abstract-level sources I could reach.)
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the most intellectually respectable finding in the whole strand, and it is the one to build the user-facing narrative around — *with two caveats*. (1) It is FX, where round numbers are shared, salient, and in a single global order book. US single-name equities have far more heterogeneous price levels, and whether the same clustering is economically material per-ticker is not established here. (2) It establishes *why a level matters to order flow*, not that a retail user can profit by trading it — see the next finding.

**CLAIM:** Published support/resistance levels from FX dealers predicted intraday trend interruptions, with predictive power persisting at least five business days, but with marked variation across firms and currency pairs.
**SOURCE:** Carol L. Osler, "Support for Resistance: Technical Analysis and Intraday Exchange Rates," *FRBNY Economic Policy Review* 6(2): 53–68, July 2000. Press summary: https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2000/rp000622a `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate (I could only retrieve the FRBNY press summary; the full PDF failed to fetch, so no effect sizes are reported here)
**Detail available:** Levels published to customers by six FX firms, 1996–98. Press release says levels were "quite successful" at predicting intraday trend interruptions; power lasted ≥5 business days after communication; all six firms could identify turning points but some were markedly better; group accuracy was higher for USD/JPY and USD/GBP than USD/DEM. **No hit rates, no magnitudes, no profitability test reported in the retrievable material.** Do not cite effect sizes from this paper without reading it.

**CLAIM:** Algorithmically identified horizontal support/resistance levels in US equities predicted trend interruptions (support better than resistance) but **failed to generate excess returns versus buy-and-hold.**
**SOURCE:** Achilleas Zapranis & Prodromos E. Tsinaslanidis, "Identifying and evaluating horizontal support and resistance levels: an empirical study on US stock markets," *Applied Financial Economics* 22(19): 1571–1585, 2012. https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/apfiec/v22y2012i19p1571-1585.html `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate (abstract-level; full text paywalled)
**Detail:** Rule-based identification from historical local extrema; ~two decades of dividend- and split-adjusted daily closes for NASDAQ and NYSE stocks. Support levels outperformed resistance levels at predicting trend interruptions. The levels did not produce excess returns versus buy-and-hold. The authors read the result as consistent with market efficiency.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the directly relevant test — US equities, automated detection — and it splits cleanly: **informative, not profitable.** That is precisely the line the app should draw between "show the level" and "score the contract on the level."

**CLAIM:** Bounce probability at intraday support/resistance rises with the number of prior bounces at the same level, in a way that shuffled-return series do not reproduce.
**SOURCE:** K. Chung & A. Bellotti, "Evidence and Behaviour of Support and Resistance Levels in Financial Time Series," arXiv:2101.07410, January 2021. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.07410 `[ACADEMIC — preprint]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak–Moderate (1 year, 3 assets, heuristic level detection)
**Detail:** Minute-level 2018 data for EURUSD, Lloyds Banking Group (LSE) and Brent. 60- or 240-minute rolling windows; band width = mean absolute price increment; Bayesian Beta estimation of p(bounce | prior bounces); permutation tests against shuffled returns. Λ (probability the original series bounces more than shuffled) exceeds 0.95 for most EURUSD and Brent cases; for the single equity (LLOY) the effect holds only up to 5 prior bounces, with the smallest estimate 0.226 — i.e. **the equity is the weakest case.** Decay: EURUSD levels with one prior bounce fall to p = 0.5 at ~350 minutes; four prior bounces, ~900 minutes. Brent one-bounce levels drop below 0.5 quickly. A unit-root AR(1) shows no memory effect; stationary AR(1) raises p(bounce) overall but gives a *negative* relation with prior-bounce count, so simple AR models don't reproduce the pattern.
**Authors' own caveats:** one year, three assets, heuristic discovery, and they explicitly do not claim a tradable edge.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** There is a real short-horizon memory effect, but it decays over *hours*, was weakest in the one equity tested, and the authors disclaim tradability. For a daily-bar, multi-day-horizon options product this is not an actionable signal. It is good material for an explainer.

**Summary position: no credible evidence of predictive or tradable value; the academic literature is thin, and what exists is either null, methodologically weak, or in markets/periods irrelevant to US equities.**

## 6. ELLIOTT WAVE

**Summary position: no credible peer-reviewed empirical validation found; no inter-rater reliability evidence found at all; the method's structure makes falsification difficult; the only forecaster track record located is poor.**

**CLAIM:** No peer-reviewed empirical study was found that validates the Elliott Wave Principle as a predictive method, using a pre-registered or out-of-sample design with a stated wave-labelling rule.
**SOURCE:** Negative result of this search (queries across journals, Scholar, SSRN, arXiv). **EVIDENCE STRENGTH: No credible evidence found.**
**Detail:** What the searches surface instead are (a) retrospective "reviews" in low-tier or predatory-adjacent outlets, (b) engineering papers that *use* Elliott labels as inputs to a neural network or fuzzy system and report in-sample accuracy without a labelling protocol or cost-aware evaluation, and (c) vendor software. Representative of category (b): Atsalakis, Dimitrakakis & Zopounidis, "Elliott Wave Theory and neuro-fuzzy systems in stock market prediction: The WASP system," *Expert Systems with Applications*, 2011 — I could not retrieve its text to assess (Semantic Scholar fetch failed), so no claim is made about its findings. Category (c) includes TradingView scripts, MQL5 indicators and WaveBasis.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Shipping Elliott wave as a component of a trend classification is shipping a layer with no empirical support of any kind. That is a product risk and a regulatory/marketing risk (see §8), not merely an accuracy risk.

**CLAIM:** No study was found measuring inter-rater reliability of Elliott wave counts — i.e. whether two trained analysts assign the same wave labels to the same chart.
**SOURCE:** Negative result of this search. **EVIDENCE STRENGTH: No credible evidence found.**
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the most important absence in the strand. Without a reliability measure, an Elliott wave label has no established measurement properties, so there is nothing for a score to be computed *from*. By contrast, pattern-detection algorithms (§1.1) and support/resistance detectors (§4) at least have reproducible definitions. **Practical test the team can run in a week:** have 3–5 Elliott practitioners independently label the same 50 charts, compute Cohen's/Fleiss' kappa on the wave-position label, and gate the feature on the result. If kappa is low, the feature cannot be a signal input and any "neutral/bullish/bearish" it produces is noise with a confident label attached.

**CLAIM:** Vendor Elliott wave software acknowledges, implicitly, that a single chart admits multiple wave counts, and does not document how validity or probability is determined.
**SOURCE:** WaveBasis, "Automatic Wave Counts" documentation. https://wavebasis.com/docs/reference-guide/automatic-wave-counts/ `[VENDOR]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak as evidence, but directly relevant as an admission
**Detail:** The documentation refers to a count "(or counts, see Alternate Wave Counts gadget)," calls its output "valid" and "high-probability" without defining either, provides no scoring or ranking method, makes automatic counts user-editable, and shifts evaluation to the user ("how accurately a wave count captures the true context of a given market"). Related settings named include "Alts for Subwave Counts" and "Wave Count Bias."
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The commercial state of the art does not claim a unique count and does not publish a probability model. An app that outputs one wave-derived verdict is making a stronger claim than the specialist vendors do.

**CLAIM:** The best-known Elliott wave forecaster's graded public forecast accuracy was 21%, against a 47% average across graded forecasters.
**SOURCE:** CXO Advisory, "Robert Prechter: 100-Year Bear?" guru grade page. https://www.cxoadvisory.com/individual-gurus/robert-prechter/ `[PRACTITIONER]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak (small sample; CXO itself flags low confidence)
**Detail:** Guru Accuracy Rating 21%, "below average"; current guru average 47%. Period Apr 2002 – Mar 2012; the published table lists 24 dated forecasts, only 5 with a visible grade, and the page does not show how 21% was computed. Method: forecasts (mostly from occasional MarketWatch columns) compared against S&P 500 returns over 21/63/126/254 trading days; complex forecasts can be marked both right and wrong. CXO explicitly notes the sample is very small and that Prechter's long horizons make independent testing difficult, so confidence is low.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Anecdotal and should be labelled as such — but it is the only track-record evidence located, and it is not favourable. In particular, the grading difficulty CXO describes (long horizons, forecasts gradeable as both right and wrong) is itself the falsifiability problem in operational form.

## 7. CANDLESTICK PATTERNS

**CLAIM:** Candlestick single-lines and reversal patterns had no forecasting power for DJIA component stocks, under a bootstrap that properly accounts for return non-normality.
**SOURCE:** Ben R. Marshall, Martin R. Young, Lawrence C. Rose, "Candlestick technical trading strategies: Can they create value for investors?", *Journal of Banking & Finance* 30(8): 2303–2323, 2006. https://econpapers.repec.org/article/eeejbfina/v_3a30_3ay_3a2006_3ai_3a8_3ap_3a2303-2323.htm · PDF: https://scispace.com/pdf/candlestick-technical-trading-strategies-can-they-create-2whgphf6vm.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** Daily OHLC for the 35 DJIA components over 1 Jan 1992 – 31 Dec 2002 (Reuters prices, CRSP dividends added back); **83,220 daily return observations.** From 18 single lines and 44 patterns in practitioner books, they removed those with no claimed predictive power and those occurring <10 times, leaving **14 single lines + 14 reversal patterns = 28 rules.** Bootstrap extends Efron (1979) to generate random OHLC series; primary null GARCH-M (results similar under random walk, AR(1), EGARCH); **500 simulations per stock**; a rule is significant at 5% if actual profits beat simulated profits in ≤25 of 500 runs. Base case: enter next day's open, hold 10 days, 10-day EMA defines prior trend.
**Results: 22 of 28 rules had no statistically significant result on any stock.** The maximum was 3 stocks (White Shooting Star). Only 6 rules were significant on ≥1 stock. Bullish single lines frequently earned *higher* profits on the random series than on actual data. Bearish rules lost money in both, with larger losses on actual data. Nine robustness scenarios (entry timing, 2- and 5-day holds, ±20% pattern parameters, 5- and 15-day MA) never exceeded 3 significant stocks. The authors note they did not even proceed to risk adjustment, transaction costs or economic significance — because nothing was significant to adjust.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the cleanest null in the strand: a large sample, a correct null model, a wide robustness grid, on exactly the market the app targets. Candlestick patterns should not be a scored signal. The detail that bullish single lines did *better on random data* is worth remembering: it shows how a naive t-test on real data manufactures apparent significance out of skew and fat tails.

**CLAIM:** A companion study confirms the null with explicit t-tests, and finds the few t-test "significances" point in the *opposite* direction to candlestick theory.
**SOURCE:** Ben R. Marshall, Martin R. Young, Lawrence C. Rose (Massey University), "Market Timing with Candlestick Technical Analysis," working paper. https://c.mql5.com/forextsd/forum/211/Market%20Timing%20with%20Candlestick%20Technical%20Analysis.pdf `[ACADEMIC — working paper]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate–Strong
**Detail:** Same 35 DJIA stocks, 1992–2002; 14 single lines + 14 reversal patterns; hold 2/5/10 days (10-day reported); entry at close the day after signal; 10-day EMA trend filter; bootstrap with 500 series per stock under random walk, AR(1), GARCH-M, EGARCH. **t-tests:** Opening White Marubozu gave significantly *negative* returns at 1% (opposite to theory); Long Black and Black Marubozu gave significantly *positive* returns at 5% (also opposite to theory). **Bootstrap:** no signal significant at conventional levels — so the t-test results were artefacts of non-normal return distributions. Consistent across two subperiods. Authors: relying on candlesticks alone "does not seem sensible."
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** A direct demonstration that the significance of a technical signal flips depending on whether you use a naive t-test or a correct bootstrap. Whatever validation the app runs must use a bootstrap or block-permutation null, not Gaussian t-tests.

**CLAIM:** An independent replication on a different developed market (Sweden) also finds no value, with hit rates indistinguishable from 50%.
**SOURCE:** Max Jönsson, "The Predictive Power of Candlestick Patterns: An Empirical Test of Technical Indicators on the Swedish Stock Market Using GARCH-M and Bootstrapping," NEKH01 bachelor thesis, Lund University, 2016. https://lup.lub.lu.se/luur/download?func=downloadFile&recordOId=8877738&fileOId=8877838 `[ACADEMIC — student thesis; lower weight]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak–Moderate (thesis, not peer-reviewed; but method mirrors Marshall et al.)
**Detail:** 29 of 30 OMXS30 constituents, 19 Oct 2007 – 31 Dec 2015; eight reversal patterns (Hammer, Hanging Man, Bullish/Bearish Engulfing, Piercing Lines, Dark Cloud Cover, Bullish/Bearish Harami); 5-day MA trend definition; one-sided predictive-power t-test, 1–10-day holding-period t-tests, and GARCH-M bootstrap with 500 series per stock. Success rates **~51% bullish, ~49% bearish**; p-values ~16–84%; Piercing Lines best at 57% but not significant. Pooled: only Piercing Lines significant, in 3 of 80 tests — attributed to chance. Individual-stock bootstrap: **50 of 2,320 comparisons (≈2%) significant**, i.e. about what you expect at a 5% threshold by chance, clustered in rare patterns and driven by outliers. Taxes and commissions excluded (so the real result is worse).
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The 2%-of-2,320 figure is a useful internal benchmark: when you test thousands of pattern/stock/horizon combinations, a few percent will pass at 5% with no effect present. Any internal "we found the ones that work" result at that rate is a false-discovery count, not a discovery.

### Disconfirming candlestick evidence (positive results)

**CLAIM:** Two-day candlestick patterns were found profitable on Taiwan 50 constituents, and the authors conclude the Taiwanese market is not efficient.
**SOURCES:** (a) Tsung-Hsun Lu, Yung-Ming Shiu, Tsung-Chi Liu, "Profitable candlestick trading strategies — The evidence from a new perspective," *Review of Financial Economics* 21(2): 63–68, 2012. https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/revfin/v21y2012i2p63-68.html — Taiwan Top 50 Tracker Fund constituents, Oct 2002 – Dec 2008; three bullish and three bearish reversal patterns; hold until an opposite-signal pattern appears; robustness across market conditions, an out-of-sample test, and a bootstrap; finding: **three bullish reversal patterns profitable.** (b) Tsung-Hsun Lu & Yung-Ming Shiu, "Tests for Two-Day Candlestick Patterns in the Emerging Equity Market of Taiwan," *Emerging Markets Finance and Trade* 48(S1): 41–57, 2012. https://ideas.repec.org/a/mes/emfitr/v48y2012i0p41-57.html — Taiwan 50 constituents, Jan 2002 – Dec 2009; four-digit classification of two-day patterns; finding: **most existing two-day patterns were not profitable, while two newly identified patterns were**; two bullish patterns consistently outperform. `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Weak–Moderate
**Why it should not change the app's design:** (1) Taiwan 2002–2009 is an emerging market in a specific regime, not US equities in 2026. (2) In (b) the profitable patterns are *newly defined by the authors* out of a four-digit classification scheme — i.e. a large search space with the winners reported, which is the exact mechanism STW and Bajgrowicz–Scaillet correct for. These papers are the honest disconfirming evidence for the candlestick null, and they are considerably weaker than the null results in §7.

## 8. IMPLEMENTATION: OPEN-SOURCE DETECTION AND ITS FAILURE MODES

**CLAIM:** TA-Lib's recursive indicators have an "unstable period" — early values depend on where the input series begins — and the library ships 61 candlestick-pattern functions whose output semantics are not documented on the reference page.
**SOURCE:** TA-Lib Python documentation. https://ta-lib.github.io/ta-lib-python/ · repo https://github.com/ta-lib/ta-lib-python `[DOCUMENTATION]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong (primary source)
**Detail:** Unstable period defaults to 0 and `get_unstable_period()` returns non-zero only after you set it yourself. `set_unstable_period()` takes an *Auto level* (`UNSTABLE_AUTO_PREC_4` / `UNSTABLE_AUTO_PREC_8`) that discards enough leading values for the first 4 or 8 significant digits to stop depending on the data start. Each function has a function-specific lookback (longer than `timeperiod` in cases such as `RSI(14)`, which needs 15 observations). 61 `CDL*` functions in the Pattern Recognition group; the reference page does not define output value semantics or sign conventions.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is a concrete, shippable bug class. If the app computes an EMA-derived indicator for ticker A on a 1-year window and for ticker B on a 5-year window, the *same bar* yields different values, and the scores are not comparable across tickers or across days. Set the unstable period explicitly, fix the lookback window length per indicator in config, and snapshot indicator values at computation time so a displayed score is reproducible. The undocumented CDL output semantics also means pattern "strength" from TA-Lib (−100/0/+100, with ±200 for some confirmed variants) must be verified against the C source before being mapped into a 0–100 score.

**CLAIM:** Pivot-confirmation detectors — ZigZag, fractals, swing-high/low — are structurally repainting: a pivot at time *t* is only confirmable using data after *t*, so any backtest that treats the pivot as known at *t* embeds look-ahead bias.
**SOURCES:** MT4 Programming, "The Algorithmic Trader's Guide to Eliminating Repainting Indicators." https://mt4programming.com/the-algorithmic-trader-s-guide-to-eliminating-repainting-indicators-how-to-stop-look-ahead-bias-from-destroying-your-capital/ · Depth Signal, "The Repainting Indicator Scam." https://depthsignal.io/blog/the-repainting-indicator-scam · PatternSmart, "Why Do Some Trading Indicators Repaint?" https://patternsmart.com/wp/why-do-some-trading-indicators-repaint/ `[PRACTITIONER — the first is a service-provider marketing piece]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong for the mechanism (it is a logical property of the algorithms); Weak for the quantitative claims
**Detail:** Repainting indicators include ZigZag (needs a later confirmed extremum to place a pivot), fractal-based scripts (same flaw), anything using TradingView's `request.security()` with `barmerge.lookahead_on`, and recalculating smoothers such as the Fisher Transform. Non-repainting: MACD, RSI, moving averages on fixed closes. The articles' quantitative claims — "up to 40% overstated simulated profits," "95% of indicators repaint" — are **not well sourced** and should not be cited. Engineering practices worth adopting: audit for `barmerge.lookahead_on`; reference confirmed bars via `[1]` offsets; in MT4/MT5 read indicator values at shift 1, never bar 0; verify by bar-replay that signals do not move after a new bar forms; prefer auditable open-source indicators over black boxes.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP — this is the single highest-risk implementation trap.** Every technique in layer 2 depends on swing points: Fibonacci needs a swing high and low; support/resistance needs confirmed extrema; Elliott wave needs a pivot sequence; classical patterns need necklines and shoulders. All of these are pivot-confirmation constructs. Unless the pipeline has an explicit confirmation lag — LMW used **3 days** (§1.1) — the backtest is using the future. And if the app *does* use a confirmation lag in production (as it must), then the levels and patterns it draws will visibly differ from the ones a user sees on a TradingView chart, which draws them with hindsight. Decide now which the product shows, and never score on a line that production cannot have known.

**CLAIM:** Decision-time leakage, as distinct from train/test contamination, inflates reported Sharpe ratios by an order of magnitude, and a simple 20-day-return momentum signal is inflated by execution-timing leakage alone.
**SOURCE:** Fan Zhang, Zhen Li, Sijia Peng, Yu Chen (University of Tokyo; HKUST-GZ), "When Alpha Disappears: A One-Switch Benchmark for Decision-Time Leakage in Financial Backtests," arXiv:2605.23959. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23959 `[ACADEMIC — preprint]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate–Strong
**Detail:** Clean reference protocol: compute signals after day *t*'s close, enter at day *t+1*'s open, label open-to-open. Clean SR@5bps ranges roughly **−0.20 to 0.68** (US panel, h=5) and **−0.01 to 0.93** (China panel) — i.e. honest performance is modest. Two single-rule violations produce enormous inflation: **EXEC_OPEN** (signal uses the full day-*t* bar including high/low/close/volume but assumes a fill at that day's open) adds ~**21.7** (US) and ~**26.2** (China) to SR@5bps for Ridge at h=5; **TEMP_CENTER** (centred instead of trailing rolling statistics) adds ~**19.4** and ~**21.7**. NORM_GLOBAL (full-sample normalisation), STRUCT_GRAPH (peer graphs using both sides of the date) and EXEC_CLOSE (same-day close entry) are weak. Effects persist across yearly windows, both markets, both horizons (5 and 20 days), and costs 0–50 bps. **Crucially for this app:** the fixed 20-day-return momentum baseline is immune to TEMP_CENTER (no rolling-centred features) but still inflated by EXEC_OPEN by ~5–6 in LG-SR@5bps. Scope: daily OHLCV large-cap equities only.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Two concrete rules. (1) **Never use a day's high, low or close in a signal that is assumed to fill at that day's open** — this alone can fabricate a Sharpe of 5+. Since classical pattern detection inherently uses highs and lows, the app must enter no earlier than the next session's open. (2) **Use trailing, never centred, rolling statistics** for any smoothing, normalisation or volatility scaling.

**CLAIM:** Perceptually important points (PIP) and rule-based pattern-definition languages are an established academic approach to automated pattern detection, but published evaluations are often qualitative rather than quantitative.
**SOURCES:** G. Zaib, U. Ahmed & A. Ali, "Pattern recognition through perceptually important points in financial time series," in *Computational Finance and its Applications* (eds. Costantino & Brebbia), WIT Press, 2004. https://www.witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/CF04/CF04024FU.pdf · Tak-Chung Fu, Fu-Lai Chung et al., "Stock time series pattern matching: Template-based vs. rule-based approaches," *Engineering Applications of Artificial Intelligence*, 2007. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0952197606001278 · "Preventing Meaningless Stock Time Series Pattern Discovery by Changing Perceptually Important Point Detection." https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221086182 `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate (as method), Weak (as evidence of predictive value)
**Detail on the 2004 paper:** the series is split into horizontal bands sized by a segmentation factor (1.5% for stock data); segments ranked by point density; foundation points found in the bottom layer, then shoulder points near the mean of earlier points with a one-time-unit tolerance, with the head placed at the mean of the two shoulders; **a head-and-shoulders match requires ≥8 matching points.** Patterns are expressed in a Pattern Definition Language parsed into search instructions. Extending the PDL requires rewriting the parser. Note the existence of a paper in this literature explicitly titled *Preventing Meaningless Stock Time Series Pattern Discovery* — the field knows that naive PIP settings generate patterns that mean nothing.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** PIP/PDL is a reasonable engineering choice for *reproducible detection*, which is strictly better than ad hoc eyeballing. But it is a detection method, not evidence of signal, and the free parameters (segmentation factor, tolerance, match count) are each a multiple-testing dimension. ## 9. METHODOLOGICAL TRAPS THE APP WILL FACE

**CLAIM:** The number of strategy variants tried determines how high an in-sample Sharpe ratio will look even with zero true skill; with 5 years of data, roughly 45 independent trials suffice to produce an in-sample Sharpe of 1.0 with a true Sharpe of 0, and with 2 years about 7 trials suffice.
**SOURCE:** David H. Bailey, Jonathan M. Borwein, Marcos López de Prado, Qiji Jim Zhu, "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism: The Effects of Backtest Overfitting on Out-of-Sample Performance," *Notices of the AMS* 61(5): 458–471, 2014. https://carmamaths.org/jon/backtest.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong (analytic result plus simulation)
**Detail:** Minimum Backtest Length upper bound **MinBTL < 2·ln[N] / E[max_N]²**. With 5 years of data, no more than ~45 independent configurations should be tried if you want to avoid an in-sample Sharpe of 1 with zero out-of-sample Sharpe; with 2 years, ~7 trials yield an expected max in-sample Sharpe of 1; N = 10 trials yields ~1.57. MinBTL is **necessary but not sufficient** — a long backtest can still be overfit. Critically, the paper shows that under "compensation effects" (a recentring constraint, or AR(1) serial dependence) in-sample and out-of-sample Sharpe become **negatively** related: the harder you optimise in-sample, the worse you do out-of-sample, even with no true trend. Recommendations: report N; raise the required in-sample Sharpe with N; reduce dependent trials to an effective independent count (e.g. via PCA); use holdouts or model sequestration.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** This is the arithmetic that governs the whole project. A per-ticker pipeline with 6 technique families × multiple pattern types × lookback windows × tolerance bands × holding horizons × score weightings runs into *thousands* of effective configurations. Against a few years of liquid-options history, the MinBTL bound says an in-sample Sharpe of 1–2 is the *expected* result from a zero-skill system. The team must log N — every variant tried, including abandoned ones — from day one. Retrofitting that count is impossible, and without it no backtest in this product can be interpreted.

**CLAIM:** After correcting for multiple testing across the published factor literature, a newly claimed predictor should clear a t-statistic of about 3.0, and most published findings in financial economics are likely false.
**SOURCE:** Campbell R. Harvey, Yan Liu, Heqing Zhu, "... and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns," *Review of Financial Studies* 29(1): 5–68, 2016 (NBER WP 20592). https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592.pdf `[ACADEMIC]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Strong
**Detail:** 316 factors catalogued from 313 published works plus selected working papers — which the authors say under-counts the true number tried, since failed unpublished tests are unobservable. Adjusted thresholds: Bonferroni/Holm at 5% FWER ≈ 3.6–3.8; BHY at 1% FDR ≈ 3.4; a minimum of ~2.8 at 5% counting only observed factors; a structural model accounting for hidden tests and correlation gives ≈ 3.9 (5% FWER) and ≈ 3.0 (1% FDR). Recommended practical hurdle: **t > 3.0** (p ≈ 0.27%). Authors note tests are unconditional and theory-derived factors deserve a lower hurdle.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Any internal signal validated at t ≈ 2 is, by the standard of the field, not validated. Set the acceptance threshold at t > 3.0 before running the first test, and apply it to the *selected* signal, not to each candidate independently.

**CLAIM:** Standard ML practice imports several specific failure modes into financial backtests, each with a named remedy.
**SOURCE:** Marcos López de Prado, "The 10 Reasons Most Machine Learning Funds Fail," working paper (first posted Dec 2017, rev. Jan 2018), drawn from *Advances in Financial Machine Learning* (Wiley, 2018). https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/SSRN-id3104816.pdf `[ACADEMIC — working paper; author has commercial interests]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate (practitioner-analytic, widely adopted)
**The ten, with the ones that bite this app in bold:** (1) Sisyphus paradigm — siloed solo strategy building → meta-strategy pipeline. (2) **Research through backtesting** — iterating models on the same data is multiple testing → use feature-importance analysis instead. (3) Chronological (time-bar) sampling → volume/dollar bars. (4) Integer differentiation destroys memory → fractional differentiation. (5) **Fixed-horizon labelling** ignores volatility and realistic exits → **triple-barrier method** (profit-take / stop-loss / time limit). (6) Learning side and size jointly → meta-labelling (direction model + act/size model). (7) **Non-IID overlapping labels** → uniqueness weighting and sequential bootstrapping. (8) **Cross-validation leakage** → purging and embargoing. (9) **Single-path walk-forward** → combinatorial purged cross-validation. (10) **Backtest overfitting** → deflated Sharpe ratio; the paper states it typically takes about 20 iterations to discover a false strategy at 5% significance.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** Items 5, 7, 8 and 10 map directly onto this product. Fixed-horizon labelling ("did the stock rise in 10 days?") is exactly how the app would naturally label pattern outcomes, and it is the wrong label for an options product with defined expiry and defined loss — the triple-barrier method, with the barriers set from the actual option's strike and expiry, is the correct formulation. Overlapping multi-day labels across a ticker universe make samples heavily non-IID, so naive cross-validation will overstate accuracy.

**CLAIM:** ML's low signal-to-noise environment plus in-sample assessment creates p-hacking pressure; the recommended safeguards are explicit.
**SOURCE:** Kristof Lommers, Ouns El Harzli, Jack Kim, "Confronting Machine Learning With Financial Research," arXiv:2103.00366. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.00366 `[ACADEMIC — preprint]`
**EVIDENCE STRENGTH:** Moderate
**Detail:** Financial data's low signal-to-noise means flexible models find spurious patterns regardless. Standard K-fold CV is biased in small samples and ill-suited to time series (cites blocked CV for stationary series, nested CV where temporal dependence is weak). Safeguards named: a revised empirical protocol (Arnott/Harvey/Markowitz 2018); combinatorially symmetric cross-validation to estimate the probability of backtest overfitting; the deflated Sharpe ratio; three-way holdout splits with embargo periods; regularisation and ensembles adjusted for non-IID data; embedding economic constraints and always benchmarking against simpler models.
**IMPLICATION FOR THE APP:** The last point is the operative one: **always benchmark against the simplest model.** For this product the simplest models are "always bullish," "buy and hold," and "random strike/expiry selection at matched delta." If the scored pipeline cannot beat those three after costs, it has no demonstrated value.

### 9.1 How a blended 0–100 score manufactures apparent skill

This is a synthesis of the above, flagged as reasoning rather than a cited finding:

1. **Aggregation launders weakness.** Six components, none with a demonstrated post-cost edge (§1–§7), do not sum to an edge. But a weighted blend produces a continuous, well-distributed score that *looks* calibrated, and a score with a smooth distribution invites the inference that it measures something.
2. **The weights are the strategy.** Choosing the six weights is itself a search over a continuous space. If the weights are tuned to make historical scores correlate with historical outcomes, the weight vector *is* an overfit model, and §9's MinBTL arithmetic applies to it — with an effective N that is large and hard to count.
3. **Signal correlation inflates apparent confidence.** Fibonacci levels, support/resistance, chart-pattern necklines and Elliott pivots are all computed from the same swing highs and lows. They are not independent evidence. A score that reads "5 of 6 signals agree → 85/100" is mostly reporting that one swing-point detector fired, five times.
4. **Selection on the visible.** Patterns are detected where they are detectable. Stocks with clean, trending, liquid price series produce more and cleaner patterns, and those stocks differ systematically from the rest — so pattern incidence is confounded with the kind of stock.
5. **Survivorship.** A current-constituent universe (today's Nasdaq-100, today's optionable names) excludes the delisted and the acquired, which is where the worst outcomes live. The Fibonacci practitioner backtest in §5 has exactly this flaw.
6. **The displayed-vs-scored gap.** Per §8, a production-safe detector must lag confirmation, so the levels the app *scores* on cannot be the hindsight-perfect levels the app *draws*. If both exist, the user will compare them and the ones they see will look better.
7. **Options compound the error.** An equity trend signal with a small information edge can still be a losing options strategy: theta decay, bid-ask spread on the contract, and the requirement to be right about *magnitude and timing*, not just direction. A directional edge of a few basis points per day cannot pay for an options spread. (Options-specific evidence is another agent's strand; this is the handoff point.)

## 11. DISCONFIRMING EVIDENCE

Evidence that runs *against* this report's broadly negative conclusions, stated at full strength:

1. **Lo, Mamaysky & Wang found real statistical structure.** Published in the *Journal of Finance*, on 34 years of CRSP data, with formal tests. All ten patterns were significant on Nasdaq under both tests, with large statistics (34–92). Dismissing chart patterns as pure noise is not supported — detected patterns did carry incremental distributional information in that era. The critique is about magnitude, horizon, costs, era and the ad hoc bandwidth — not about the existence of structure. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/0022-1082.00265

2. **Savin, Weller & Zvingelis found ~5–7%/yr risk-adjusted excess returns** from H&S-conditioned positions combined with the market portfolio, in a peer-reviewed econometrics journal, on S&P 500 and Russell 2000 data. I could not retrieve the full text to check robustness or costs. If those returns are net and robust, the chart-pattern verdict above is too harsh. **This is the single most important open item in the strand.** https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jfinec/v5yi2p243-265.html

3. **Sullivan, Timmermann & White confirmed BLL's results survive data snooping in-sample.** Their best rule returned 18.65%/yr over 90 years with a snooping-adjusted p of 0.000 — and BLL's own rule stayed significant across all four in-sample subperiods. That is a real, formally-corrected result. The adverse finding is confined to 1987–1996. STW themselves offer three explanations and note that an incomplete rule universe biases adjusted p-values *toward* zero, which cuts in TA's favour for the in-sample period. https://www.kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf

4. **Park & Irwin's count is 58 positive vs. 24 negative.** The majority of 92 modern studies found profitable technical strategies. One can argue selection and publication bias, but the raw literature does not read as uniformly negative, and the survey authors stop short of saying TA does not work — they say the question is not settled. https://farmdoc.illinois.edu/assets/marketing/agmas/AgMAS04_04.pdf

5. **Osler (2003) supplies a genuine, non-behavioural-handwaving mechanism for support/resistance,** grounded in observed order-book data, and it explains *both* of TA's canonical level predictions. This is better than "it works because people believe it." A product that builds on this specific finding is standing on real ground. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1540-6261.00588

6. **Osler (2000) found dealer-published support/resistance levels predicted intraday reversals, with power persisting ≥5 business days** — a longer horizon than the Chung & Bellotti hours-scale decay would suggest. I could not retrieve effect sizes, so this cuts against my §4 characterisation and needs checking. https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2000/rp000622a

7. **Candlesticks are not universally null.** Lu, Shiu & Liu (2012) and Lu & Shiu (2012) find profitable two-day patterns in Taiwan with out-of-sample and bootstrap checks. The null results are from the US and Sweden; market-specific inefficiency is a live possibility, and the app's thesis could in principle hold for a different universe than the one that has been tested. https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/revfin/v21y2012i2p63-68.html

8. **Trend-following has survived as a real business for decades.** Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen report a net Sharpe of 0.77 over 1880–2013 and 0.62 over 2000–2013, with −0.30 S&P correlation. It is vendor research with hindsight construction and the authors' own extensive caveats, but managed-futures AUM is not an illusion, and the Sepp & Lucic independent estimate (net Sharpe ~0.5 on an SG Trend Index benchmark of 0.47) is in the same range. Trend following is the one technique here with both academic and commercial survival. https://oxfordstrat.com/coasdfASD32/uploads/2016/03/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf

9. **Gray's steelman of TSM is fair:** one-month-ahead returns are so noisy that even a genuine predictor would struggle to clear a bootstrap threshold, so Huang et al.'s failure to reject the null is not the same as establishing absence. And Huang et al. explicitly say they are not claiming predictability is absent, only that a constant 12-month rule is unsupported. https://alphaarchitect.com/are-trend-following-and-time-series-momentum-research-results-robust/

10. **Chung & Bellotti's result is not explainable by a simple AR process.** Unit-root AR(1) shows no memory effect, and stationary AR(1) produces the *wrong sign* on prior-bounce count. Something real is happening at support/resistance levels that standard time-series models don't reproduce. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.07410

11. **"No evidence found" is not "evidence of absence" for Fibonacci and Elliott wave.** The honest reading is that these techniques have not been well tested, largely because they resist formalisation — not that they have been tested and failed. A team that built a *reproducible* Elliott labeller with a published inter-rater kappa and a pre-registered out-of-sample test would be generating genuinely new evidence. That is a research project, though, not a shippable feature.

## 12. OPEN QUESTIONS

1. **Savin, Weller & Zvingelis (2007) full text** — are the 5–7%/yr risk-adjusted excess returns net of transaction costs? What is the holding horizon? How were the analyst-derived filters chosen, and does the result hold with the unfiltered LMW detector? Has anyone extended it past 1999? **This is the highest-value unresolved item.**
2. **Tsinaslanidis & Voukelatos, "Automatic identification and evaluation of Fibonacci retracements" (*Expert Systems with Applications*, 2021)** — the one rigorous academic test of Fibonacci levels I located, and I could not retrieve it. Does it include a random-level control? **Read before any Fibonacci feature is specified.**
3. **Osler (2000) effect sizes** — the FRBNY press release says levels were "quite successful" but gives no hit rates. What were the actual reversal frequencies versus a random-level benchmark, and did she test profitability net of spread?
4. **Does round-number order clustering transfer to US single-name equities and to listed options strikes?** Osler's mechanism is FX-specific. Options strikes are themselves round numbers, which creates a pinning/clustering channel that might be stronger and more measurable than anything on the underlying's chart. Worth a dedicated search; overlaps another agent's strand.
5. **Inter-rater reliability of wave counts and of pattern identification** — no study found for either. A small original study (3–5 practitioners, 50 charts, Fleiss' kappa) would be cheap, novel, and decisive for the Elliott and chart-pattern features.
6. **Has any post-2010 US-equity study replicated LMW's pattern informativeness?** The newest relevant work found here is 2012 (Zapranis & Tsinaslanidis, support/resistance). Decimalisation, HFT, zero commissions and 0DTE options have all reshaped the microstructure since LMW's sample ended in 1996.
7. **Is there any pattern-conditioned result in the *options* domain** — e.g. does implied volatility or skew behave differently after a detected pattern, independent of the underlying's direction? A pattern could be worthless for direction and still be informative about realised-vs-implied volatility, which is a defensible options thesis. Not covered by this strand.
8. **Marshall, Young & Rose replicated post-2002?** Their candlestick null ends in 2002. Twenty-four further years exist.
9. **Chang & Osler (1999) full paper** — Park & Irwin report 13% and 19%/yr H&S returns for mark and yen, with the authors concluding H&S use was *not rational* because simpler rules dominated. Worth reading directly; it is the cleanest example of "a positive return that is still not a reason to use the technique."
10. **What does the SEC/FINRA position imply for a scored technical verdict delivered to retail options users?** Not a research question for this strand, but the combination of (a) unvalidated signals, (b) a numeric 0–100 confidence presentation and (c) an options recommendation is the shape of problem that attracts regulatory attention. Flagging for the product/legal track.

## 14. BOTTOM LINE FOR THE PRODUCT

Layer 2 as specified contains **one technique with a real mechanism** (support/resistance, via round-number order clustering — informative, not profitable in US equities), **one with contested but non-trivial academic standing** (momentum/trend, defensible only as a relative cross-sectional rank or a long-horizon regime tag, not as a per-ticker directional call), **one with weak and era-bound evidence** (classical chart patterns), **one with a strong published null on exactly this market** (candlesticks), and **two with no credible supporting evidence of any kind** (Fibonacci retracements, Elliott wave — the latter also lacking any measurement-reliability evidence, so there is nothing to score).

The defensible product shape that follows: **draw everything, score almost nothing.** Chart annotation, level drawing and pattern labelling are legitimate as *visualisation and education* — users want them, they are honest as descriptions of what the price did, and the support/resistance explainer has a real citable mechanism behind it. What is not defensible is converting those drawings into a number that implies predicted direction, then feeding that number into an options recommendation. The validated-signal budget in layer 2 is close to zero, and a blended 0–100 score is the specific mechanism by which near-zero signals get presented as confidence.

Three non-negotiable engineering constraints, regardless of what ships:
- **Confirmation lag on every pivot-derived construct.** LMW used 3 days. Without it, the backtest reads the future, and every one of the six techniques is pivot-derived.
- **Never let a signal that uses day-*t*'s high, low or close assume a fill at day-*t*'s open.** Per Zhang et al., this alone fabricates a Sharpe above 20.
- **Log N — every configuration tried, including abandoned ones — from the first commit.** Per Bailey et al., without N no backtest in this product can be interpreted, and the count cannot be reconstructed later.

Market and contracts

Stage 3 opens the option chain for the first time. It takes the frozen thesis from stage 2, builds option structures that express the view, describes each on the same terms, and ranks them by a priority the user selects. The first-ranked structure is the recommendation. The stage does not say whether the trade is worth taking.

1. Overview

A view says where the underlying is expected to finish. A trade needs more than that: a structure, an expiry, strikes, and a price. This stage supplies them by fixed rules and sets the resulting candidates side by side, so that the user chooses among them on stated terms.

What the stage receives

The frozen thesis from stage 2: the underlying, the view, the primary horizon, whether the view depends on a dated catalyst, the usual range and volatility state, the risk flags, and chart levels. No option price has been seen up to this point.

What the stage does not do

It does not judge whether the view is right or the trade worthwhile. It does not set position size, which belongs to stage 5. It does not place orders. No agent takes part: every strike, expiry and figure is computed in code.

What the stage produces

ComponentDefinition
CandidatesA small set of defined-risk option structures that express the view: spreads, a single bought option, or an iron condor, each with its expiry and strikes.

Market-implied chance of profitThe chance of profit that current option prices imply, shown beside the base-rate figure as context.
ExposureThe candidate's net delta, theta and vega.
ChecksWhether earnings fall before expiry, and whether a dividend falls before expiry on a short leg. Shown on the row; they set nothing aside.
Ranking and recommendationThe candidates in order, by a priority the user selects. The first-ranked candidate is the recommendation, with the reason stated.
  • The recommendation serves a stated priority. The user selects what matters most: matching the view, a larger payoff, or the lowest trading cost. The system ranks on that measure. The recommendation is not a forecast and does not state that the trade is worth taking.
  • Defined risk only. Every candidate has a known maximum loss. Spreads, iron condors and single bought options qualify. Uncovered short options are not proposed.
  • Cost is measured on the structure. Two legs that each pass the liquidity gate can still form a spread that is expensive to trade relative to its price. The figure that matters is the cost of the whole structure.
  • The user decides. The user may take the recommended candidate, an alternative, or none.

Procedure

  1. Confirm the frozen thesisThe thesis record from stage 2 is read as written. It cannot be changed from this point; an amendment creates a new record.
  2. Open the option chainLive quotes are used when the market is open. When it is closed, the most recent snapshot is used and every figure is marked provisional.
  3. Re-apply the liquidity criteriaThe user's stage 1 settings are applied to the chain just retrieved, without the days-to-expiry criterion. Only eligible contracts may form part of a candidate. For a spread or an iron condor the bid-ask criterion is judged on the whole structure in step 7, not on each leg. There is no override at this stage.
  4. Choose the expiryThe default is the eligible expiry nearest the horizon date of the thesis, among those on or after it or up to three trading sessions before it. The user may switch to an expiry that serves another horizon from the research brief.
  5. Build the candidatesStructures are assembled by fixed rule from the view, with strikes placed by reference to the usual range. Each spread is priced in both of its constructions, with calls and with puts. A directional view also produces a single bought option.
  6. Describe each candidatePayoff, the two history-based chances, the market-implied chance, trading cost and exposure are calculated for each, on the same basis.
  7. Apply the filtersEach candidate's trading cost is tested against the cap. A candidate over the cap is set aside with the reason shown; a candidate that could not be built from eligible contracts is listed with its reason. Events before expiry are shown on the row and set nothing aside.
  8. Rank and recommendThe remaining candidates are ranked by the priority the user selects. The default is to match the view. The first-ranked candidate is the recommendation.
  9. User selectionThe user selects a candidate or none. The expiry, the priority, the ranking and the selection are written as decision records, and the selected candidate passes to stage 4.

2. Building the candidates

Candidates are assembled by fixed rule. The view determines the kind of structure, the usual range determines where the strikes sit, and the horizon determines the expiry. Nothing in this step involves judgement.

From the view to the candidates

Each directional view produces two spreads and a single bought option. One spread requires a move in the view's direction. The other requires only that the underlying does not move against the view. The single option is a call for a bullish view and a put for a bearish view, bought at the price. A range-bound view produces an iron condor at two widths.

  • Two constructions, one payoff. Each spread can be built with calls or with puts at the same strikes. The payoff line does not move when the construction changes; only the legs and the premium do. The system prices both and keeps the one that costs less to trade.
  • The two shapes are different trades. A spread that requires a move pays more and pays less often. A spread that requires no adverse move pays less and pays more often. The system sets both beside each other; it does not treat them as interchangeable.

Placing the strikes

  1. Measure the usual range for the expiryThe band that held 68% of past moves over the number of days to expiry. In the example, ±5%, or $95 to $105.
  2. Anchor at the priceThe first strike is the eligible strike nearest the current price. A single option is bought at that strike, and its row states how far the strike is from the price.
  3. Place the second strike one usual range awayAbove the price or below it, according to the shape, moved to the nearest eligible listed strike. Where that is not one usual range away the row states the distance used. If no eligible strike exists on that side the candidate is not built, and the screen says why.
  4. For an iron condor, sell at the edges and buy beyondThe short strikes sit at the edges of the usual range, or at 1.25 times it for the wider version. Each long strike is the next eligible strike beyond.

Choosing the expiry

The expiry is the eligible one nearest the horizon date, among those on or after it or up to three trading sessions before it; when two are equally near, the one on or after is chosen. Change the horizon to see which expiry is chosen. The rule for a view that depends on a dated catalyst (the expiry must fall after that date) is not applied in the first version: the thesis record carries no catalyst date.

Why these rules, and what is provisional
  • Why defined risk only. Every position must have a known maximum loss, so that the user can decide how much to put at risk. An uncovered short option has no fixed limit.
  • Single bought options. The maximum loss is the premium paid.
  • Why strikes come from the usual range. It is the one measure of likely movement the system has counted from the underlying's own history, and it scales with the days to expiry.
  • Why only eligible contracts. A structure with one illiquid leg is expensive to enter and harder to leave.
  • What makes a leg eligible. A valid two-sided quote, and the user's open-interest, minimum-premium and strike-distance settings. The days-to-expiry setting is left out, because the expiry comes from the thesis horizon. For a spread or an iron condor the bid-ask setting is not applied to each leg: an option a usual range from the price is cheap, so a quote a few cents wide is a large share of its premium, and applying the setting leg by leg pulled the strikes in or kept only the dearer construction. The cost of the whole structure is judged instead, by the cost cap. A single bought option keeps the bid-ask setting. Where a leg is quoted wider than the setting, the detail says so.
  • When the expiry falls well after the horizon. If the nearest eligible expiry is some days beyond the horizon, the screen states by how many. The payoff figures then describe the position at expiry, not at the horizon.
  • Provisional choices. The strike distances (one usual range, and 1.25 for the wider condor) and the width of the condor's wings are design choices.

3. Describing the candidates

Every candidate is described on the same measures, calculated in code from the chain and from the underlying's price history. The same measures appear as columns on the output screen, so candidates can be compared line by line.

Pays when the view is right

Answers: if the researched view proves correct, does this structure pay? A structure that needs a large move can lose even when the direction was right.

Base-rate chance of profit

Answers: with no view at all, how often has this structure paid? It is the figure to compare with the market-implied chance.

  • No expected return is shown. The system claims no edge, so a fairly priced structure loses its trading cost on average. The screen states what the underlying must do for the trade to pay.
  • Both history-based figures are blended. As with the base rate in stage 2, a single stock's own history is combined with that of all single stocks, using the same weights. Because stocks differ in how far they move, each past move is first divided by that stock's usual range, and the break-even is expressed the same way. The blended figure is shown and the stock's own figure is kept in the record. Index funds use their own history.
  • The history flatters bullish structures. Price history contains only companies that survived. Both history-based figures inherit that bias.
  • Delta is not shown as a probability. It is shown only as exposure.
  • Chart levels are context. The 60-day high and low of the daily close are drawn on each candidate's payoff chart, and one sentence states where the break-even sits relative to them. They do not place a strike, set aside a candidate or affect the ranking.
  • Chart levels are measured. Each candidate's position relative to the levels is saved in the record. Once enough trades have resolved, candidates with a level on their side are compared with the rest. Levels gain a role in placing strikes only if that comparison supports it.

4. Filtering and ranking

Filters decide which candidates may be shown as choices. The ranking then orders those that remain, according to a priority the user selects. Both are fixed rules in code.

Filters

FilterA candidate is set aside whenSetting
LiquidityA leg cannot be found among eligible contracts: the candidate is not built, and is listed with the reason.The user's stage 1 controls, without days to expiry; the bid-ask setting applies to a single option only
Trading costThe cost of the whole structure exceeds the cap.Share of the amount at risk. Default 5%, adjustable on the screen from 2% to 10%
EventsNothing is set aside. Where earnings fall before expiry, or a dividend falls before expiry on a short leg, the row is marked and the challenge in stage 4 carries the flag.Shown, not filtered. A control to include or exclude such candidates is deferred
  • Set aside, not hidden. A candidate that fails a filter is listed beneath the comparison with the reason and a label in place of its rank. It may be opened for inspection and cannot be selected.
  • Within a payoff shape, the cheaper construction is kept. The other construction has the same payoff, so it is shown inside the detail of the one kept and is not ranked separately.
  • If no candidate remains, the screen says so and shows why each was set aside.

Ranking by a chosen priority

The remaining candidates are different trades, so no single ordering is correct for every purpose. The user selects what matters most and the system ranks on that measure alone.

PriorityRanked byFavours
Match the view defaultPays when the view is right, highest first.Structures that pay whenever the direction is right, whatever the size of the move.
Larger payoffWhat the candidate pays if the underlying moves one usual range in the direction of the view, as a multiple of its maximum loss, highest first. For a range-bound view, what it pays if the underlying finishes where it started.Structures that pay more for a move of ordinary size, and pay less often.
Lowest costTrading cost as a share of the amount at risk, lowest first.Structures that lose least to the bid and ask.
  • Why "larger payoff" is measured at one usual range. A bought option has no limit on its gain, so ranking on maximum gain would place it first every time, whatever the prices. One usual range is a measured quantity and gives every structure a finite figure on the same basis. A bought option keeps gaining beyond that point; the screen says so, and the ranking does not reward it.
  • Ties are settled by lower trading cost, then by fewer legs.
  • The first-ranked candidate is the recommendation, with the reason stated in a sentence. Changing the priority changes the recommendation, and the priority in force is recorded.
  • The recommendation is not a forecast. It identifies the candidate that best serves the chosen priority. It does not state that the trade is worth taking.
  • Gross richness is never a ranking measure. No candidate is ranked on how expensive its options appear.

The user selects the recommended candidate, another candidate, or none. Every decision taken in the stage is written as a decision record, and the records are retained in memory if the contract is traded.

What is handed to stage 4

  • The selected candidate, with its legs, quotes and measures, and the proposed number of contracts. The final size is confirmed in stage 5.
  • The thesis record from stage 2, with any horizon amendment attached.
  • The candidates not selected, so that the challenge can ask why a different structure was not preferred.
Why the stage ranks by a chosen priority
  • The candidates are not equivalent. Two constructions at the same strikes differ only in trading cost, and there the cheaper is simply better. A spread that needs a move, a spread that needs no adverse move and a single option are different trades. No evidence available to the system shows that one of them earns more.
  • Why "Match the view" is the default. The researched view states a direction and states no size of move. The structure most faithful to that view is the one that pays whenever the direction is right.
  • Why single options are included. A bought call or put has a known maximum loss, the premium paid. It is the simplest expression of a directional view and the least expensive to trade.
  • Why chart levels do not place strikes. No measurement available to the system shows that these levels hold, and any definition of a level involves arbitrary choices. Letting them move a strike would change every figure on the screen on the strength of an untested input. Showing them and recording the outcome tests the idea at no cost. The general definition of a level is still to be settled; the first version uses the 60-day high and low of the daily close.
  • What is not built. Uncovered short options, because the loss has no fixed limit. Cash-secured puts and covered calls, because they commit the price of the shares. Structures across two expiries, because their payoff cannot be stated at a single date.
  • Why a cost cap as well as the liquidity gate. The gate tests each contract alone. A spread's net price is much smaller than either leg, so two contracts that each pass can form a structure that costs a large share of the amount at risk to trade.
  • The cost cap is a control. The 5% default is a design choice, confirmed by the user as an adjustable setting. Counted on live chains on 19 November 2026 across 33 views: 35 of 72 candidates were over the cap at 5%, 54 at 2% and 15 at 10%. The control was put on the screen for that reason. After the bid-ask setting stopped being applied leg by leg, 27 of 86 were over the cap at 5%.
  • The poor-match mark is a warning only. Confirmed by the user. The factor of two is a design choice. Estimating a position's value at the horizon, which would remove the need for the warning, is deferred: it requires a pricing model and assumptions about volatility.

Known data gaps. Earnings dates carry no time of day, so the report day counts as before expiry. A future ex-dividend date appears only once it is declared; until then the screen says "none declared". Symbols the data does not cover show "not available".
